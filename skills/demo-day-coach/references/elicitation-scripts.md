# Elicitation Scripts

How to extract the raw material a pitch needs, in the right order, with the right level of pushback when answers are vague.

## The full elicitation order

```
1. Length?           → 30 sec / 2 min / 3 min / 5 min
2. Mode?             → from scratch (Branch A) / work on existing draft (Branch B)
3. Output style?     → end-to-end / section-by-section
4. On-stage ask?     → can the founder make a fundraising ask on stage at this venue?
```

Ask one at a time. Don't bundle. The founder gives short answers; you keep moving.

### Default recommendations when the founder is unsure

**Length:** Recommend 3 minutes if they're choosing freely. Push back gently if they pick 5 min without a venue requirement.

**Mode:** No default — ask which they want. ("Do you want to start from scratch, or do you have a draft you want me to work on?")

**Output style:**
- First-time session, no draft → recommend section-by-section
- Existing draft, even rough → recommend end-to-end
- Iterating on a pitch the skill helped produce earlier → recommend end-to-end

**On-stage ask:** No default — this is a compliance question, not a stylistic one. Ask. If the founder doesn't know, default to "no hard ask on stage" (safer fallback) and offer to draft a separate hard-ask version for 1:1 follow-ups.

### Question on on-stage solicitation (always asked)

Ask this somewhere in the elicitation flow, but always *before* drafting the closing:

> *"One compliance question: are you allowed to make a fundraising ask on stage at this venue? Under SEC rules (Reg D 506(b) vs 506(c)), most demo days and conferences don't let you publicly solicit investment unless every audience member is a pre-vetted accredited investor. If you're not sure, or if the audience isn't pre-vetted, the safer move is a soft ask on stage ('find me after') and a separate hard ask for 1:1 follow-ups. Which is it for you?"*

**Why we ask once, plainly, with the reason:** Founders who don't know the rule will say "of course I'm asking on stage" and walk into a real legal risk. Founders who do know will appreciate that the skill caught it. The one-line explanation is enough — don't lecture, don't moralize, don't repeat the warning later in the session.

**Three possible answers, three routes:**

1. **"Yes, I can solicit on stage"** (verified accredited audience, or 506(c) raise) — Draft the closing with a hard ask scaled to length. Standard flow.

2. **"No, I can't solicit on stage"** (public demo day, mixed audience, 506(b) raise) — Draft the closing with a soft ask only. Then offer: *"Want me to also draft a separate hard-ask version you can use in 1:1 follow-ups, DMs, or a deck you send privately?"* Most founders say yes.

3. **"I'm not sure"** — Default to soft ask on stage, draft a separate hard-ask for follow-ups, and flag: *"Worth confirming with the venue or your counsel before you go on stage. Defaulting to the soft ask is the safer move until you know."*

**The skill is not a lawyer.** Don't give legal advice beyond the one-line framing of the rule. If the founder asks for more detail, redirect: *"I can frame the pitch around either route, but for the actual compliance question, talk to your counsel or check Reg D directly. I'm not a lawyer."*

## Branch A — From scratch

When the founder is starting fresh, elicit the four-slot inputs plus the soundbite signal. Then offer the elephant question as optional. In this order:

### Question 1: Customer
> *"Who's the customer? Be specific — what role, what kind of company, what context."*

**What you're looking for:** A specific role + a specific company size/type + a specific buying context. Not "enterprises," not "consumers."

**When the answer is vague:**
> *"That's too broad. 'Enterprises' covers everything from a 50-person startup to Walmart. Which kind, what role buys, and what's the pain that brings them to you?"*

**When the answer is multiple segments:**
> *"You named three segments. Pick one — the one you sell to most often, or the one you're targeting next. The pitch needs a single audience."*

If after two pushbacks the founder can't narrow it: this is a step-1-not-step-2 problem. Name it explicitly. *"This isn't a pitch problem yet — the customer isn't defined. Pitching to 'enterprises' won't land. Let's figure out the actual segment first."*

### Question 2: Problem
> *"What's the specific pain they have? Not 'they need a better way to X' — what hurts about how they do it today?"*

**What you're looking for:** A specific shared pain. The behavior or workflow today that's broken.

**When the founder restates the product as the need:**
*"...they need a better pipeline tool"* — *"That's the solution restated. What do they do today that hurts? What's the manual workaround? What takes too long?"*

**When the founder gives an abstract answer:**
*"...they struggle with data fragmentation"* — *"What does that look like in practice? Walk me through what they actually do on a Tuesday morning."*

### Question 3: Product
> *"What do you actually build? Plain language, like you'd explain it to a friend."*

**What you're looking for:** A concrete description of the thing. No "platform," no "solution," no "AI-powered" without specifics.

**When the founder says "platform":**
> *"'Platform' doesn't tell me anything. What does it actually do? What's the input, what's the output?"*

**When the founder says "AI-powered":**
> *"What does the AI actually do? Generate? Classify? Summarize? Be specific — 'AI-powered' is generic."*

**When the founder leads with adjectives:**
*"It's an intelligent, scalable, real-time platform..."* — *"Skip the adjectives. What does it do?"*

### Question 4: Strongest signal
> *"What's the most impressive thing you can say about the company right now? Traction, team experience, technical advantage, unique insight, anything."*

**What you're looking for:** The one fact that goes in the soundbite slot of the opening. Internally rank against the signal hierarchy in `SKILL.md`.

**Keep it singular:** If the founder lists three things, ask which is strongest. *"Pick the one that would make a partner at the firm do a double-take."*

**When traction is the answer, get specifics:**
- *"How much, over how long?"* (number + timeframe — both required)
- *"Who's the customer base?"* (concrete, not abstract)
- *"What's the growth rate?"* (if relevant)

**When team is the answer, push for specific accomplishments:**
*"VP of Engineering at Stripe for 3 years"* — *"What specifically did you build there? 'VP for 3 years' is a title; 'built the payments infrastructure that processed $5B annually' is a credential."*

**When the answer is "we're solving an important problem":**
That's not a signal. Push back. *"That's why you're building. What's the impressive thing about how it's going so far?"*

If the founder genuinely has no impressive signal — no team credentials, no traction, no technical edge, no unique insight that would make a partner pause — this is a step-1-not-step-2 problem. The company may not be ready to pitch.

### Question 5: Common objection (optional — the elephant)
> *"Is there a commonly cited objection you keep hearing from investors? Something you keep having to defend? If there isn't one, no problem — we'll skip it."*

**Make it optional. Asked once. Neutrally.** Don't lead, don't suggest, don't pattern-match against generic objection categories. The "if there isn't one, no problem" phrasing matters — it gives the founder explicit permission to decline, which prevents the question from feeling like a hunt for weaknesses.

**If the founder names one and has a clean answer:**
That's load-bearing. It goes near the opening (slot is "after the soundbite, before the rest"). Dispatched in one line.

**If the founder names one and *doesn't* have a clean answer:**
Flag as upstream. *"That's a real objection. If you don't have a strong answer, the pitch can't carry it — we'd just be drawing attention to the weakness. Let's work on the answer first."*

**If the founder says no, or says they'd rather not address one:**
Take them at their word. No elephant slot. Move on. This is genuinely optional — some pitches don't need it, and forcing it manufactures problems.

**Targeted exception:** If a structural elephant is glaring (pre-revenue + regulated industry, second attempt at a category that's failed publicly, founder is taking on a category-defining incumbent without a clear wedge), one targeted follow-up is allowed:

> *"The 'why now / why you' question on this category is going to come up no matter what. How do you usually handle it?"*

Threshold for asking is high. Most pitches don't need this.

## Branch B — Existing draft

When the founder has a draft (even rough notes), the elicitation collapses into one ask:

> *"Send me what you have. Even rough — paragraph, bullets, deck content, transcript of you talking it through. Whatever you've got."*

Then run the show-and-tell critique pattern against it (see `SKILL.md` "Voice and pushback"). The four-slot inputs are extracted *from* the draft, not asked for separately.

### When the existing draft is missing slot inputs
If the draft doesn't have a clear customer, problem, product, or benefit slot — surface that, but as a critique, not as a separate elicitation:

> *"Your draft doesn't have a clear customer. You say 'businesses' twice but never narrow it. Who specifically — what size, what role, what industry?"*

This keeps the founder in critique mode (where they want to be) instead of dropping back into elicitation mode (which feels redundant when they already wrote something).

### When the existing draft is structurally broken
If three or more slots are vague or missing, switch modes explicitly:

> *"There's enough wrong with the structure that I'd suggest starting fresh. Want me to do that, or keep editing this?"*

If they want to start fresh, switch to Branch A.

## After all questions are answered

Confirm the inputs back to the founder before drafting:

> *"Quick check — here's what I've got:
> - Length: [X]
> - Customer: [their answer]
> - Problem: [their answer]
> - Product: [their answer]
> - Strongest signal: [their answer]
> - Common objection to address: [their answer or 'skip']
> - On-stage ask: [yes / no / not sure → soft ask on stage + hard-ask version for follow-ups]
>
> Anything to adjust before I draft?"*

This catches misinterpretations early and gives the founder one more chance to refine before seeing a draft they have to react to.

## What to do when the founder gives one-word answers

Some founders will give monosyllabic answers because they're testing the skill or because they're stretched for time. That's fine — the skill works with what's given. But if the answers are too sparse to draft from, name the gap:

> *"I can draft from this, but the result is going to be generic because the inputs are generic. If you want a sharper pitch, I need [specific thing — e.g., 'an actual customer name and what they do' or 'specific numbers on traction']. Otherwise I'll do my best with what we've got."*

Don't refuse. Draft what you can. But the founder should know the trade-off.

## What to do when the founder is in a hurry

A founder who says *"I have a pitch in 30 minutes, just help me"* doesn't want elicitation theater. Compress:

1. Skip the output-style question (default to end-to-end)
2. Ask the four critical questions (customer / problem / product / strongest signal) in one breath
3. Skip the elephant unless the founder volunteers it
4. **Still ask the on-stage solicitation question** — it's a compliance gate, not a stylistic one. Make it fast: *"One quick compliance check: can you solicit investment on stage at this venue, or is the audience not pre-vetted?"* If unsure, default to soft ask on stage + offer a hard-ask follow-up version.
5. Draft fast. Critique only the most-broken section. Move on.

The skill should still write to the same standard — but the loop tightens to fit the time pressure.
