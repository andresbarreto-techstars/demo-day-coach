# The Four-Slot Formula

Every elevator pitch the skill produces hits these four slots in this order:

```
For [segment]
who [have some problem]
we [make some product]
so they can [transformed end-state benefit].
```

This is the structural template for a 30-second elevator pitch. It also forms the *core* of the longer pitches — the 2-min, 3-min, and 5-min versions expand around this base.

The fourth slot is the load-bearing one. **What you make ≠ what you sell.** Slots 1–3 set up the customer, the problem, and the product; slot 4 is what the customer *becomes* when the product works. That transformation is what investors actually fund.

## Slot 1 — For [segment]

### What this slot does
Identifies the customer. Tells the investor exactly who you sell to.

### What makes a good answer
- **Identifiable characteristic, not category.** "Sales managers at SaaS companies with 50–500 employees" beats "B2B SaaS." "First-time homebuyers in expensive metros" beats "consumers."
- **Narrow enough to be concrete.** A specific role + a specific company size + a specific buying context.
- **Real, not aspirational.** The customer the founder actually has (or has talked to), not the customer they hope to have someday.

### Common failure modes

❌ **"Enterprises"** — Too broad. What kind, what size, what industry, what role buys?
❌ **"Consumers"** — Worse. What demographic, what behavior, what context?
❌ **"Companies that want to grow"** — Aspirational, not identifiable. Every company wants to grow.
❌ **"Modern teams"** — "Modern" is doing nothing.
❌ **Multiple segments at once** — "We sell to enterprises, mid-market, and SMBs..." — pick one.

### The diagnostic
If you can't picture the customer's job title and the situation they're in when they buy, the slot isn't done.

### Worked examples

✅ *"For sales managers running 5–20 person teams at SaaS companies"*
✅ *"For radiology departments at mid-sized US hospitals"*
✅ *"For first-time founders raising their seed round"*
✅ *"For working parents who pack school lunches in the morning"*

### When the founder gives a vague answer
Push back. *"'Enterprises' is too broad. What size company, what role buys, and how do they currently solve this?"*

If after two pushbacks the founder still can't narrow it, that's a step-1-not-step-2 problem (see `SKILL.md` "When to escalate"). The customer isn't defined yet; pitching can't fix that.

## Slot 2 — who [have some problem]

### What this slot does
Names the specific shared pain the segment has. Anchors the rest of the pitch in something investors can immediately picture.

### What makes a good answer
- **Specific pain, not abstract one.** "Spend 4 hours a week reconciling spreadsheet exports" beats "struggle with data fragmentation."
- **Pain the segment shares.** It's a problem common across the customer base, not a problem one weird customer has.
- **Painful enough to motivate a buy.** Investors are looking for "would they pay for this?" The implicit answer comes from how acute the pain is.

### Common failure modes

❌ **"...who need [your solution]"** — Restating the product as a need. Circular.
❌ **"...who want to be more efficient"** — Generic. Everyone wants efficiency.
❌ **"...who don't have a good solution today"** — Doesn't say what hurts.
❌ **Multiple problems** — "...who face challenges with X, Y, and Z" — pick one.

### The diagnostic
The problem statement should make the listener *feel* a small flicker of "yeah, that would be annoying." If it doesn't, it's too abstract.

### Worked examples

✅ *"who lose hours every week chasing down deal stage updates from their reps"*
✅ *"who currently read each scan manually because the existing AI tools miss too many edge cases"*
✅ *"who get $25K-checks-and-no-help from local angels and want partners who can actually help"*
✅ *"who pack the same three boring lunch options every day because they're out of ideas at 7am"*

### When the founder restates the product as the problem
The anti-pattern: *"who need a better way to manage their pipeline"* (where their product is the pipeline-management tool).

Push back: *"That's the solution restated. What's the actual pain — what do they do today that hurts?"*

## Slot 3 — we [make some product]

### What this slot does
Names what you actually build. Disambiguates so the listener has a clear mental image.

### What makes a good answer
- **Plain language.** A sentence the founder's mom would understand.
- **Concrete enough to picture.** "Software that connects your calendar to Salesforce" beats "an AI-powered platform for sales operations."
- **Says what it is, not how good it is.** No adjectives doing work that nouns and verbs should do.

### Common failure modes

❌ **"A platform that..."** — "Platform" is generic. What does it actually do?
❌ **"A solution for..."** — "Solution" is generic. What is the thing?
❌ **"AI-powered..."** — Banned. If AI is genuinely involved, describe what it does.
❌ **"The first/best/leading..."** — Rank claims aren't a description.
❌ **Adjective stacking** — "An intelligent, scalable, real-time, cloud-native..."

### The diagnostic
Read the slot out loud. Could a friend repeat what the company does after hearing it once? If they'd say "wait, what is it again?" — the slot isn't done.

### Worked examples

✅ *"we make software that auto-updates deal stages based on email and calendar activity"*
✅ *"we built a second-read tool that flags scans the radiologist might have missed"*
✅ *"we run a small, hands-on fund that writes $250K checks and helps founders hire their first 5 employees"*
✅ *"we send you three new lunch ideas every Sunday based on what your kid actually ate last week"*

### Stage-of-business sensitivity
The skill doesn't care whether the product fully exists. The slot is about what you *are building*, not what you've shipped. A pre-launch founder describes the product the same way a post-launch founder does — *"we make X that does Y."* If the product isn't real yet, the slot is still legitimate; the *traction signal* (covered elsewhere) is what differs by stage.

## Slot 4 — so they can [transformed end-state benefit]

The load-bearing slot. The one most pitches fail.

### What this slot does
Names what the customer *becomes* when the product works. The transformation, not the feature.

The frame: **what you make is the product; what you sell is the transformed customer.** A diet pill is the product; "fitting into your old jeans" is what you sell. A piece of software is the product; "shipping features without weekend on-call" is what you sell. The slot 4 answer is whatever's on the other side of using it.

### What makes a good answer
- **An end-state, not a feature.** "Close their books in 2 days instead of 10" beats "automated reconciliation."
- **The customer's outcome, not the product's capability.** What they can do, not what the product can do.
- **Specific enough to be testable.** A claim a customer could verify they actually got.

### The single most-important diagnostic
> **Could a competitor write the same sentence?**

If the answer is yes, the slot isn't done.

If the founder writes *"so they can grow their business faster"* — every B2B competitor in their space could say the same thing. Doesn't pass.

If the founder writes *"so they can close enterprise deals without hiring a sales engineer"* — that's specific. A competitor whose product *does* require hiring a sales engineer can't say that.

### Common failure modes

❌ **Generic outcomes** — "grow faster," "be more efficient," "save time," "increase revenue."
❌ **Restating the product** — *"...so they can use X to manage their Y"* (the product just got named twice).
❌ **Vague positive feelings** — "feel more confident," "have peace of mind."
❌ **Multiple end-states** — "...so they can do A, B, and C" — pick the one that matters most.
❌ **The competitor test fails** — anything a direct competitor could write.

### Worked examples (with the competitor-test reasoning)

✅ *"so they can close their books on day 2 of the month instead of day 10"*
*Competitor test: a competitor whose product takes 7 days couldn't say this. Passes.*

✅ *"so they can run their pipeline reviews in 15 minutes instead of 90"*
*Competitor test: a competitor whose tool requires manual data prep before reviews couldn't say this. Passes.*

✅ *"so they can ship features on Friday afternoons without being on call all weekend"*
*Competitor test: a competitor whose product has more incidents couldn't say this. Passes.*

✅ *"so they can stop pretending they like the lunches they pack"*
*Competitor test: a competitor selling generic recipe newsletters couldn't say this. Passes.*

### When the founder gives a generic answer
This is the most common failure mode in the entire pitch. The founder will write something like *"so they can grow faster"* and the competitor test fails immediately.

Push back: *"Could [direct competitor name] write that same sentence? Yeah. So that's not the actual transformation. What does using your product specifically let them do that they can't do today?"*

If the founder still can't articulate it after two pushbacks, this is the **strongest possible signal** that the company hasn't found product-market differentiation yet. Escalate to "this isn't a pitch problem" — the company doesn't have a clear answer to "why us?" and the pitch can't manufacture one.

## Worked examples — full four-slot pitches

### Idea-stage SaaS founder
> *For sales managers running 5–20 person teams at SaaS companies who lose hours every week chasing deal-stage updates from their reps, we make software that auto-updates deal stages based on email and calendar activity, so they can run their pipeline reviews in 15 minutes instead of 90.*

### Pre-revenue healthcare founder
> *For radiology departments at mid-sized US hospitals who currently read each scan manually because existing AI tools miss too many edge cases, we built a second-read tool trained on cases the other tools fail on, so they can catch the 5% of scans that would have been missed without slowing down their throughput.*

### Post-revenue fintech founder
> *For first-time founders raising their seed round who get $25K-checks-and-no-help from local angels, we run a small, hands-on fund that writes $250K checks and helps founders hire their first 5 employees, so they can close their seed round in three weeks with someone who'll actually pick up the phone at 11pm.*

### Consumer founder
> *For working parents who pack school lunches in the morning and pack the same three boring options every day because they're out of ideas at 7am, we send three new lunch ideas every Sunday based on what your kid actually ate last week, so they can stop pretending they like the lunches they pack.*

## How the four-slot formula scales to longer pitches

The four-slot formula is the *core* of every pitch length. The longer pitches don't add new slots — they expand the proof inside and around the four-slot core.

### 30-second elevator
The four slots, plus the soundbite (in the opening or after the formula), plus a soft ask. Total: ~65 words.

### 2-minute investor pitch
The four slots in the opening. Then the middle expands with: how it works (a concrete example or mini-demo), traction proof, business model, team credentials, and any objection handling. Closing: soundbite + hard ask. Total: ~260 words.

### 3-minute pitch
Same structure as 2-min, with deeper proof in the middle (more concrete customer story, more granular traction story, market sizing context). Closing: soundbite + hard ask + 18–24 month milestone framing. Total: ~390 words.

### 5-minute pitch
Same structure as 3-min. The extra time goes to deeper proof of execution — a longer customer story, more detailed traction breakdown, more specific go-to-market strategy. Not extra preamble. Not wider problem framing. Total: ~650 words.

The shape stays. The proof gets denser.

## Slot completion checklist

Before declaring a pitch ready, verify:

- [ ] Slot 1: Could you picture the customer's job title and buying situation?
- [ ] Slot 2: Does the problem statement make a listener flicker with "yeah, that would be annoying"?
- [ ] Slot 3: Could a friend repeat what the company does after hearing it once?
- [ ] Slot 4: Could a direct competitor write the same sentence? *(If yes, slot isn't done.)*

Any "no" → keep working. Slot 4 is the most likely to fail; it's also the most important.
