# Opening and Closing

The two memory slots. Investors decide in the first 20 seconds and remember the beginning and the end. The middle is reinforcement. Get these two right and the pitch carries; get them wrong and nothing in the middle saves it.

## The opening (the first 20 seconds)

### The default shape

```
We're [Company]. We [do X plain-language] for [who].
[Strongest soundbite — traction OR team OR tech].
[If applicable: elephant named and dispatched in one line.]
```

That's three sentences, ~50 words at most. At 130 WPM, that lands in the 20-second window with room to breathe.

### What each line is doing

**Line 1: company name + what it does + who it's for.**
Plain spoken English. No "we are revolutionizing X" / "we are the leader in Y" / "we're building the future of Z." Just: name, action, audience.

**Line 2: the soundbite.**
The single most-impressive thing the founder can credibly say about the company. This is what gets repeated to partners. See "Soundbite selection" below.

**Line 3: the elephant (only if the founder named one).**
A pre-emptive one-liner that defuses the obvious objection before it can contaminate the gut decision. Short. No defensiveness. *"I know what you're going to ask: how do we compete with [incumbent]? [One-line answer.]"*

### Worked examples across stages

**Idea-stage founder (great team + fresh insight; no traction yet):**
> *"We're Gradient. We help mid-market sales teams forecast their pipeline more accurately. I built the forecasting system at HubSpot that powered $400M of bookings — and the thing nobody else has noticed is that the deal-stage data most CRMs collect is actively misleading."*

Soundbite: the credential ("built the forecasting system at HubSpot that powered $400M of bookings"). Elephant note: this founder might also need to address "but you're early" — if they do, the third line could be *"We're four months in with three design partners; this is the right moment to invest because the moat is the data we're collecting now."*

**Pre-revenue founder (waitlist + technical advantage):**
> *"We're Quill. We turn meeting recordings into structured product specs for engineering teams. We have 1,200 product managers on the waitlist after a single demo video — most of them at companies you've heard of."*

Soundbite: the demand signal (waitlist with a credible quality marker — "companies you've heard of"). No elephant unless the founder named one.

**Post-revenue founder (paying customers):**
> *"We're Beacon. We replace the manual review work in mortgage underwriting. We hit $2M ARR in our first 14 months, and our top customers are doubling their volume with us this quarter."*

Soundbite: the revenue plus the expansion signal. Two beats of traction, both real.

**Growth-stage founder (rapid growth — the strongest red rung):**
> *"We're Marbl. We do payroll for hourly workers in retail. We've grown from $300K to $4M ARR in 11 months and we just signed our first 10,000-employee customer."*

Soundbite: the growth rate plus the breakthrough deal. The growth rate alone is enough; the deal is the cherry.

### What an opening should never look like

❌ *"In today's hyperconnected world, businesses are struggling to keep up with the pace of change..."*
(Throat-clearing opener. Out.)

❌ *"Imagine if every salesperson on your team had a coach in their ear..."*
(Theatrical hook + rhetorical premise. Out.)

❌ *"After 15 years in enterprise software, I noticed a problem..."*
(Personal-story opener. Out.)

❌ *"The CRM is broken."*
(Problem-first opener — investor is left wondering what you do for two more sentences. Out.)

❌ *"We're a next-generation AI-powered platform that transforms how teams collaborate."*
(Six banned words in one sentence. Out.)

### The opening fits in 20 seconds

At 130 WPM, the budget is roughly 43 words for the first 20 seconds. The default shape above lands in 35-50 words depending on how much fits in the soundbite line.

If the opening is over 50 words, cut. The most common offenders:
- Two sentences explaining what the company does instead of one
- Adjective stacking ("intelligent, scalable, cloud-native...")
- A second sentence on the audience ("for B2B SaaS companies, particularly in the mid-market...")

## Soundbite selection

The soundbite is the single thing the investor will repeat to their partners. It comes from the founder's strongest signal.

### How to pick

When the founder lists what they have, silently rank against the internal signal hierarchy in `SKILL.md`. The top-ranked claim is the soundbite. Ties break toward whichever is most concrete and specific.

### What makes a soundbite work

- **Concrete numbers when possible.** "$400K ARR in 6 months" beats "early traction." "1,200 on the waitlist" beats "strong demand."
- **A credibility anchor.** A name a partner would recognize, a metric a partner can verify, a specific accomplishment with a specific company.
- **Singular, not plural.** One soundbite. Don't pile two into the opening — the second one gets lost.
- **The same one used at the close.** Repetition across the two memory slots is what locks it in.

### When the founder has multiple strong signals

Pick one. Save the others for the middle as reinforcement. If a founder has revenue + a great team + a technical edge, the opening leads with revenue (highest-ranked signal); the team and the tech show up in the middle.

### When the founder has weak signals across the board

The substitution hierarchy applies (see `SKILL.md` and the "No traction case" section). Lead with whatever is strongest. If nothing reaches a credible bar, escalate to "this isn't a pitch problem" — the company isn't ready to pitch.

## The closing

### The default shape

```
[Soundbite — same as the opening one]
[Ask — specific, instrumented]
```

Two sentences. The soundbite first (engraves the memory). The ask second (the very last thing they hear is what triggers the next action).

### Why soundbite first, ask last

The very last sentence is the most-retained. The ask is what the investor will *act on* — the reason they walk up to the founder vs. walk past. So:

- Soundbite = what they'll *remember* (and repeat to partners)
- Ask = what they'll *do* about it

Reversing the order leaves the ask floating without the anchor of why-it-matters.

### Ask templates by length and on-stage status

The ask scales on two axes: pitch length, and whether the founder is allowed to make a fundraising solicitation on stage (see `elicitation-scripts.md` for the on-stage question).

#### When on-stage solicitation IS allowed

This means the audience is verified accredited (e.g., a 506(c) raise, an investor-only event with pre-vetted attendees) — or the founder has confirmed it with the venue.

**30-second elevator pitch — soft ask:**
> *"If you want to see the numbers, find me after."*
> *"If this resonates, let's talk."*
> *"Happy to walk you through the data when you have a minute."*

The 30-sec pitch's job is to earn the next conversation, not close a check. A soft ask works.

**2-minute investor pitch — hard ask:**
> *"We're raising $1.5M on a SAFE at a $12M cap to hit $2M ARR by Q4 next year. $250K minimum checks. Closing by [specific date]."*

Specific dollar amount. Specific instrument. Specific outcome the money buys. Specific minimum check size. Specific close date.

**3-minute pitch — hard ask + outcome framing:**
> *"We're raising $4M to get from 12 hospitals to 100 by end of next year, pacing to $10M ARR by month 18. SAFE at a $30M cap, $250K minimum, closing in six weeks."*

The outcome framing tells the investor what milestone the money buys, which tells them what valuation step they're underwriting. *Not* "to hire 30 people and lease office space."

**5-minute pitch — same as 3-minute, plus deeper context:**
The 5-minute pitch's extra time goes to deeper proof of execution in the middle, not extra preamble. The ask doesn't change shape — same outcome framing, same specifics. The closing remains tight.

#### When on-stage solicitation is NOT allowed

This is the default at most demo days, conferences, and meetups where the audience isn't pre-vetted as accredited investors. Under SEC Reg D 506(b), making a public fundraising solicitation in this context is a real legal problem — not a stylistic one.

**All lengths — soft ask only on stage:**
> *"If you want to see the numbers, find me after."*
> *"If this resonates, come find me afterward — happy to walk through the details."*
> *"More to share — find me after the panel."*

No dollar amount on stage. No instrument. No cap. No close date. The soundbite carries the memory; the soft ask earns the 1:1.

**Then offer to draft a separate hard-ask version** for use in 1:1 follow-ups, private decks, DMs, or any non-public solicitation channel. That version follows the on-stage-allowed templates above. Frame it explicitly so the founder knows which is which:

> *"Here's the on-stage close — soft ask only. And here's a hard-ask version for the conversations afterward, when you're 1:1 with someone you've confirmed is an accredited investor. Don't read the second one from the stage."*

### Outcomes, not uses of funds — the load-bearing rule for the ask

The ask names what the money *gets the company*, not what the company will *spend it on*. This is the difference between an investable claim and a budget.

**Why it matters:** Investors don't underwrite line items. They underwrite the next valuation step — what milestone makes the company worth more in 18–24 months. The ask either tells them that or wastes the slot.

✅ Outcomes (good):
- *"Raising $3M to hit $5M ARR from signed LOIs by end of next year."*
- *"Raising $2M to launch in three new markets and reach 50K paying users."*
- *"Raising $5M to get FDA clearance and our first 10 hospital deployments."*
- *"Raising $4M to reach default-alive at $300K MRR."*

❌ Uses of funds (banned):
- *"Raising $3M to hire 8 engineers and 3 GTM people."*
- *"Raising $2M for 18 months of runway."*
- *"Raising $4M for product development, marketing, and team expansion."*
- *"Raising $5M to scale our infrastructure and grow the team."*

**Diagnostic:** *"Could the founder substitute a different set of hires or spend categories and still hit the same outcome?"* If yes, the outcome is what belongs in the ask — the inputs are interchangeable, the milestone isn't.

If a founder pushes back on this rule (some founders learned a use-of-funds template from a YC deck or a fundraising book), push back twice — once explaining why outcomes outperform inputs in the ask, once with a side-by-side rewrite of their ask. After that, comply with a flag: *"You can frame it your way. The outcome version lands harder with most investors — happy to keep both versions and you can A/B them across pitches."*

### Things never to put at the end

❌ *"And we're just getting started."*
❌ *"The future is bright."*
❌ *"Thank you for your time."* (fine to say, but not the last *substantive* line — engineer the order so soundbite-then-ask happens, then the polite "thank you" closes)
❌ Any new information the investor has to process. The end is for engraving what's already been said.
❌ Uses-of-funds framing in the ask.
❌ A hard fundraising ask on a stage where on-stage solicitation isn't allowed.

### Bad asks

❌ *"What do you think?"* (banned question)
❌ *"If you're interested, let's talk."* (no specifics)
❌ *"We'd love your support."* (begging, not asking)
❌ *"We're looking for partners who believe in our vision."* (filtering for sycophants)
❌ *"Raising $3M to hire 8 engineers and build out our GTM team."* (uses of funds, not outcomes — the investor doesn't know what they're underwriting)
❌ *"Raising $5M for 24 months of runway."* (runway is an input; outcome is what the runway buys)

### Good asks

✅ *"Raising $2M on a SAFE at a $15M cap to hit $3M ARR by Q4 next year. $100K minimum check, closing in eight weeks. If you're a fit, find me after."*
✅ *"We need $3M to hit $5M ARR by Q4 next year. SAFE at a $20M cap, $250K minimum. Three slots left."*
✅ *"$500K seed round to get to 10K paying users and the metrics that unlock our A. Holding two slots open for strategic partners. If that's you, let's talk."*
✅ *"More to share on the round — find me after."* (when on-stage solicitation isn't allowed)

## Repetition between opening and closing

The soundbite at the open and the soundbite at the close should be the **same one**. Sometimes phrased identically, sometimes paraphrased — but the same fact.

**Why:** The two memory slots reinforce each other. An investor walking out can repeat one fact to their partners; if it's been said twice, they have it.

**Example pattern:**
- Opening soundbite: *"We hit $2M ARR in our first 14 months."*
- Middle: explanation of how, who's buying, what's working
- Closing soundbite: *"Two million ARR in 14 months, and our top customers are doubling their volume this quarter."*

Same fact, slightly different framing. The repetition is the point.

## Common opening / closing failures and their fixes

| Pattern | What's wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Opening with "the problem is..." | Investor doesn't know what you do yet | Lead with what you do; problem comes after |
| Opening with personal story | Founder backstory underperforms credentials | Cut. Use one-line credential in team slot |
| Opening with three things at once | No clear soundbite | Pick the strongest one. The other two go in the middle |
| Opening that's longer than 50 words | Too long for the 20-sec window | Cut. Adjective stacking and second sentences are the usual culprits |
| Closing with "thank you for your time" as the last line | Wastes the recency slot | Move "thanks" before the soundbite-then-ask, or drop it |
| Closing without a specific ask | Investor has no action to take | Add specific dollar amount + instrument + close date (length-appropriate) |
| Soundbite at open, different soundbite at close | Memory slots don't reinforce each other | Use the same fact twice |
| Closing introduces new claims | Audience can't process new information at the end | Move new claims to the middle. End repeats what's already said |
